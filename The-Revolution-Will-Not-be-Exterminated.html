Preface1970
Gil Scott-Heron, and the Irony That Followed Him

The revolution will not be televised.
Now it is livestreamed, and nothing changed.

In 1970, Gil Scott-Heron recorded "The Revolution Will Not Be Televised," a spoken-word piece arguing that political transformation is not a broadcast product. It will not arrive as a rerun, will not be sold between commercials, will not be something a citizen consumes from a couch while the actual work of changing a society happens somewhere else, to someone else, on someone else's schedule.[1]

Every case study in this document inverts the literal premise while leaving the argument intact. Nepal's 2025 uprising was coordinated on a Discord server with over 100,000 members.[24] India's 2026 movement was born on a single social media platform within a day of the insult that named it.[8] Iran's 2022 protests were documented in real time by ordinary phones as state television refused to acknowledge them existed. The revolution is now filmed constantly, from every angle, by the people living inside it. Visibility was never the actual constraint. This document is an attempt to identify what the constraint has been instead, across ten cases spanning fifty years and five continents, using the language a state reaches for when it decides a citizen is not a citizen but a pest.

[1] Scott-Heron, G. (1970). "The Revolution Will Not Be Televised." Small Talk at 125th and Lenox. Flying Dutchman Records.
The Mechanism1928
Karl Mannheim, and Why the Clock Resets Every Time

Nobody inherits the last generation's scar tissue.
Everyone has to grow their own.

In 1928, the sociologist Karl Mannheim published "The Problem of Generations," arguing that political consciousness is not transmitted like property. It is formed, once, by whichever crisis a cohort happens to be young during. A generation that comes of age during economic collapse, foreign occupation, or state violence forms a durable political identity around that specific event, regardless of whether older generations already fought and won or lost an equivalent battle.[2]

This is the mechanical reason the pattern in this document repeats rather than accumulates. Soweto's schoolchildren in 1976 did not benefit from institutional memory transmitted by a prior youth uprising against apartheid, because there had not yet been one on that scale. Nepal's Gen Z protesters in 2025 were not meaningfully briefed by Sri Lanka's 2022 uprising or Bangladesh's 2024 uprising, despite happening in the same region within three years of each other and being explicitly compared by outside observers as a single regional pattern.[23] Each generation is issued its own grievance from scratch. Each state, in response, reaches for the same three tools: dismissal, then the baton, then, years later, a retroactive claim that it always tolerated dissent.

[2] Mannheim, K. (1928). "Das Problem der Generationen." Kölner Vierteljahrshefte für Soziologie 7, 157–185, 309–330. English translation in Mannheim, K. (1952). Essays on the Sociology of Knowledge. Routledge & Kegan Paul.
Specimen 0116 June 1976
Periplaneta afrikaansae
Soweto Uprising
Soweto, South Africa

On 16 June 1976, thousands of Black schoolchildren in Soweto marched in protest against a government directive mandating Afrikaans, the language of the apartheid state, as the medium of instruction for core subjects in Black schools. Police opened fire on the student march. Hector Pieterson, thirteen years old, was among the first killed; a photograph of his body being carried through the street became the defining image of the day.[3]

Specimen 028 July 2016
Periplaneta pellettum
Kashmir Unrest
Kashmir Valley, Indian-administered Jammu & Kashmir

The killing of Hizbul Mujahideen commander Burhan Wani, 22, in an encounter with Indian security forces on 8 July 2016 triggered an eight-month wave of youth-led street protests, funeral processions, and stone-pelting confrontations across the Kashmir Valley. Security forces responded with pellet-firing shotguns, a weapon marketed as non-lethal, deployed against crowds that were disproportionately composed of teenagers and men in their early twenties.[4]

Specimen 0417 Dec 2010
Blattella maghrebi
The Arab Spring
Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, Syria, Yemen, Bahrain

On 17 December 2010, Mohamed Bouazizi, a 26-year-old fruit vendor in Sidi Bouzid, Tunisia, set himself on fire after municipal officials confiscated his cart and allegedly humiliated him publicly, the latest in a chain of routine harassment of a young man with no other path to income. His death on 4 January 2011 detonated protests that removed President Zine El Abidine Ben Ali within weeks, then cascaded into Egypt, where eighteen days of occupation of Tahrir Square ended Hosni Mubarak's thirty-year presidency, and further into Libya, Syria, Yemen, and Bahrain.[6]

Specimen 0518 Oct 2019
Blattella metropolitana
El Estallido Social
Santiago, Chile

On 18 October 2019, a 30-peso increase in Santiago's metro fare, raising the price from 800 to 830 pesos, prompted secondary-school students to organize mass fare evasion, which escalated within hours into the largest civil unrest Chile had seen since the return to democracy. The protest's own chant supplied the essay's clearest single data point on how a specific grievance functions as a proxy for accumulated decades of inequality: "No son 30 pesos, son 30 años", "it is not 30 pesos, it is 30 years," referencing the three decades since Pinochet's dictatorship ended in 1990 without a corresponding change to the economic model it left behind.[7]

Specimen 06Mar–Jun 2019
Periplaneta victoriae
Anti-Extradition Bill Movement
Hong Kong Special Administrative Region

Proposed legislation permitting extradition to mainland China triggered demonstrations beginning in March 2019 that, by June, produced marches organizers estimated at close to two million participants, roughly a quarter of Hong Kong's population. The movement's stated position, "five demands, not one less," expanded from opposing the specific bill to demanding an independent inquiry into police conduct, amnesty for arrested protesters, and universal suffrage.[8b] The bill was formally withdrawn in September 2019, but Beijing imposed a National Security Law on Hong Kong in June 2020, criminalizing much of the movement's remaining activity and its leadership structure.

Specimen 08Jul–Aug 2024
Periplaneta quotae
The Quota Reform Uprising
Dhaka and nationwide, Bangladesh

University students began protesting in July 2024 against a job quota system reserving a majority of government positions for descendants of 1971 independence-war veterans, a system perceived as a mechanism for rewarding political loyalty to the ruling Awami League. Specimen 098 Sept 2025
Blattella discordia
The Gen Z Uprising
Kathmandu and nationwide, Nepal

On 4 September 2025, Nepal's government banned 26 social media platforms, including Facebook, WhatsApp, and Instagram, citing failure to comply with local registration rules. The ban, layered onto existing anger over corruption and a viral "Nepo Kids" campaign exposing the lifestyles of politicians' children, ignited protests on 8 September in which police killed nineteen people in Kathmandu within hours, using tear gas, rubber bullets, and live fire on crowds Human Rights Watch later determined the police had fired on indiscriminately.[11] Prime Minister K.P. Sharma Oli resigned the following day. With no formal leadership structure, the movement organized its transition to an interim government through a Discord server exceeding 100,000 members, using polls and dedicated fact-checking sub-channels to reach consensus on a nominee.[24]

Specimen 1015 May 2026
Periplaneta janta, type specimen
The Cockroach Janta Party
New Delhi and nationwide, India

On 15 May 2026, India's Chief Justice, Surya Kant, remarked from the bench during a Supreme Court hearing that "there are youngsters like cockroaches, who don't get any employment or have any place in the profession," referring to unemployed young people who had become active as media commentators, activists, and RTI petitioners. This is the only case in this document where the vocabulary of extermination was not inferred or reconstructed after the fact. It is the literal, on-the-record origin of the movement's name.[8]

Within a day, digital strategist Abhijeet Dipke launched the Cockroach Janta Party on social media as satire, with membership criteria listed as being "unemployed, lazy, chronically online, and able to rant professionally." The movement's satirical premise collapsed into a real, sustained political mobilization within weeks, converging with existing student anger over the 2026 NEET medical entrance exam paper leak and CBSE on-screen marking irregularities, and drawing activist Sonam Wangchuk into a hunger strike beginning 28 June. On 20 July 2026, more than 10,000 marchers attempting to reach India's Parliament were met with tear gas and baton charges.[12]

FindingsCross-Case
Why "Vermin," Specifically, Recurs Across Unrelated States and Decades

The word is not an insult.
It is a legal argument about who counts as a person.

The philosopher and cognitive scientist David Livingstone Smith has documented, across historical and contemporary case studies, that describing a group as vermin, parasites, or infestations is not incidental rhetorical excess. It performs a specific cognitive function: it removes the target from the category of beings whose deaths require moral accounting, while leaving their capacity to cause harm (infestation, contamination, spreading) fully intact as a justification for forceful removal.[13] This is distinct from ordinary political insult. Calling an opponent corrupt, foolish, or dangerous still concedes they are a political actor whose claims must be answered. Calling them vermin concedes nothing; it reframes the encounter as pest control rather than negotiation.

None of the ten cases in this document required the state to use the word "cockroach" literally to perform this function. Mehbooba Mufti describing Kashmiri youth as "instigated," Chinese state media describing Tiananmen's students as "counter-revolutionary rioters," Iranian state media describing the Woman, Life, Freedom protesters as foreign-orchestrated, all perform the identical rhetorical operation: reassign political actors into a category (the manipulated, the criminal, the foreign-controlled) whose claims do not require an answer. India's Chief Justice Kant is the outlier only in having used the actual biological word, on the record, in a country whose highest court, rather than a street-level official, made the comparison.

[13] Smith, D.L. (2011). Less Than Human: Why We Demean, Enslave, and Exterminate Others. St. Martin's Press.
Counterweight2011
Erica Chenoweth, and the Empirical Case That This Is Not Hopeless

No government, ever tracked,
has withstood 3.5% of its own population.

Political scientist Erica Chenoweth and Maria J. Stephan's 2011 study of over 300 violent and nonviolent campaigns between 1900 and 2006 found that campaigns achieving active, sustained participation from at least 3.5% of a country's population have, without exception in their dataset, succeeded in their stated political objective. Nonviolent campaigns in their sample succeeded roughly twice as often as violent ones.[14] The finding is now commonly cited as the "3.5% rule," though Chenoweth has repeatedly cautioned it is a descriptive threshold observed in a historical dataset, not a guaranteed law.

Several cases in this document sit close to or above that threshold. Egypt's Tahrir Square mobilization is estimated to have directly involved several percent of the national population at its peak. Chile's 25 October 2019 march alone represented over 6% of the national population in a single city on a single day. This does not guarantee a favorable outcome, as Chile's subsequent constitutional rejection demonstrates, but it is the strongest available empirical counterweight to the assumption that youth-led mobilization is structurally doomed against a state with more force at its disposal.

[14] Chenoweth, E. & Stephan, M.J. (2011). Why Civil Resistance Works: The Strategic Logic of Nonviolent Conflict. Columbia University Press.
ConclusionSynthesis
Three Endings, in Sequence

Televised, but not watched.
Forgotten, but not defeated.
Still, somehow, crawling.

First. Every case documented here was, in its own era's terms, more visible than any prior generation's uprising. Soweto had a single photograph. Nepal had a hundred-thousand-person Discord server and a live feed from every phone in Kathmandu. Visibility increased by orders of magnitude across these fifty years, and the underlying pattern, dismissal, then force, then years of institutional silence, did not soften proportionally. Gil Scott-Heron's literal claim, that revolution would not arrive as television, has been disproven by the historical record. His actual claim, that watching was never going to be the mechanism of change, has not been touched. The revolution is now filmed constantly and remains, in most of these ten cases, unresolved or only partially resolved for the people who lived it.

Second. The deeper failure is not the state's. It is the absence of transmission between generations that Mannheim identified in 1928 and that this document's own timeline confirms case by case. Nepal's Gen Z protesters were not meaningfully briefed by Sri Lanka's 2022 collapse or Bangladesh's 2024 uprising, despite occurring in the same region within three years. Kashmir's 2016 unrest carried no institutional memory forward to India's 2026 movement, despite both involving young people confronting the same state's security apparatus a decade apart. Each generation relearns, from the beginning, that dismissal precedes force precedes silence, at the cost of its own dead. This document exists as one small, partial attempt to interrupt that specific failure, by placing ten cases side by side so the pattern becomes visible as a pattern rather than ten unrelated tragedies.

Third, and last. The cockroach, as a species, is not remarkable for strength. It is remarkable for surviving conditions engineered specifically to kill it, generation after generation, indefinitely. Every state in this document built its own version of the extermination apparatus: apartheid classrooms, pellet guns, tanks, water cannons, platform bans, morality police, and, in India's case, a sitting judge's own vocabulary. None of it has yet produced a generation that declines to try again. Soweto's schoolchildren did not stop apartheid in 1976. It ended in 1994. The eighteen years between those dates were not evidence the students failed. They were the incubation period. This document does not end on optimism, because optimism was never the claim. It ends on the more modest and better-supported observation that the thing being called vermin has, in every single case catalogued here, kept crawling forward regardless of what it was called.
