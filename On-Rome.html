ON ROME

The first Roman was a murderer. His victim was his twin. We do not know if Romulus struck the actual blow; the sources waver between versions almost from the beginning, and the one archaeological claim to have found his original wall remains contested rather than confirmed.[1] But I am less interested in what happened on the Palatine that day than in what the Romans chose, for the next thousand years, to keep telling each other about it. They could have said the city was founded by a god, by a treaty, by a council of elders. Plenty of ancient cities said exactly that. Rome said it began with a brother who could not share power.

Close your eyes. Marble temples. Legions in lockstep. A gladiator's blade catching the light before the emperor's thumb decides which way it falls. Good. Hold that image. Now I am going to show you the rot beneath it.

That gladiator is not a hero. He is property, re-enacting the founding trauma for a crowd that cannot stop watching brothers kill brothers. That marble was paid for with provincial grain taken from families who never consented to Rome's name on their harvest. And the emperor is only the latest survivor of a game that began the moment Romulus looked at Remus and saw a rival where a treaty would have found a brother.

Act I · The WoundRomulus to the Early Republic
Founding · Abduction · Bondage

Rome Was Not Built
on Grandeur.

It was built on a single, unhealed wound, repeated at every scale the city ever reached.

01 ~ The Origin Code
Romulus & Remus

Livy tells it as a dispute over auspices, a quarrel about whose omen counted, that ends with Remus dead and Romulus alone on the Palatine.[1] Plutarch is less tidy, offering several competing versions circulating even in antiquity, in one of which Romulus does not deliver the blow himself but does not stop it either.[2] T.P. Wiseman's study of the myth's political afterlife argues persuasively that the fratricide was not simply inherited folklore. It was retold, reshaped, and re-emphasized precisely during the periods when Rome was tearing itself apart in civil war, a myth the Romans kept reaching for whenever they needed to explain, to themselves, why brothers in a republic kept killing each other.[3] I find this more disturbing than a simple founding murder. A city that keeps a fratricide as its origin story is not confused about its own nature. It is confessing.

02 ~ The First External Theft
The Rape of the Sabines

Rome, founded by refugees, criminals, and unmarried men, had a demographic problem it solved the way it solved most problems: by taking what it needed from the outside and calling the result a marriage.[1] Livy's account frames the abduction as a regrettable necessity later redeemed by the Sabine women themselves, who intervene to stop the resulting war between their fathers and their new husbands.[1] I am not interested in redemption arcs supplied by the very historians writing for the empire that benefited. I am interested in the mechanism: a city that reproduces itself not by generation alone but by abduction, absorption, and the retroactive renaming of theft as union. This is not an isolated incident in the founding period. It is the founding period's operating principle, stated once, in the clearest possible terms, before Rome had the size to disguise it as policy.

03 ~ The Institutional Extension
Slavery

The paterfamilias did not only govern his blood children. Under the doctrine of patria potestas, he held, in principle, the power of life and death over every member of his household, including bodies he had not fathered but had captured.[4] Keith Hopkins's demographic study of the Roman slave system estimates that by the late Republic, slaves constituted somewhere between one third and two fifths of the population of Italy, a proportion sustained overwhelmingly through conquest rather than reproduction.[5] William Harris's later work on the sources of the Roman slave supply confirms the pattern: war, not breeding, was the primary mechanism, meaning the household, the familia, was not merely a kinship unit with an unfortunate exception carved out for captives. It was, at scale, an absorption system for outsiders, industrialized. The logic of the Sabine women did not stay a myth. It became an economy.

Act II · The Theatrical RepetitionThe Republic and the Arena
Spectacle · Bondage Repeated · Scale

Make It Spectacle,
and It Ceases to Be Terror.

The arena is not a break from the founding wound. It is the wound, rehearsed nightly, for an audience that paid for the privilege.

01 ~ The Masterstroke
Gladiators

The munus, the funeral games from which gladiatorial combat descended, were originally an offering to the dead, blood spilled at a grave to honor and, one suspects, to placate the deceased.[6] Kathleen Coleman's study of Roman executions staged as mythological re-enactments demonstrates something the arena's defenders rarely admit outright: Roman spectacle did not merely display violence, it scripted violence into inherited narrative, restaging old stories with new, expendable bodies.[7] Alison Futrell's account of the arena as an instrument of political religion goes further, arguing that the games functioned as a ritual technology for managing exactly the kind of fratricidal anxiety Rome had carried since Romulus, converting an unmanageable communal trauma into a scheduled, ticketed, survivable event.[8] Watch two men who share a barracks, a trainer, sometimes a friendship, fight to the death for entertainment, and ask yourself what story that is actually retelling. I think the answer is obvious enough that the Romans built an entire industry around not asking it aloud.

02 ~ The Crime, Re-Assigned
Slavery, Again

Gladiators were, in the overwhelming majority, slaves, condemned criminals, or prisoners of war, trained by the state or by private investors to perform combat that free Roman citizens were nominally forbidden from performing themselves.[5] Carlin Barton's psychological study of the gladiator's social position describes a figure held simultaneously in contempt and a kind of horrified admiration, infamis under the law yet capable, through skill or an honorable death, of a prestige no ordinary slave could touch.[9] The state took outsiders, broke whatever family bonds they arrived with, and assigned their bodies the specific labor of re-enacting the crime the city itself had been founded on. If Romulus killing Remus is the wound, the gladiator is the scar tissue the empire kept re-opening on purpose, in public, at scale, for cash.

03 ~ The Bridge
The Punic Wars

Rome's wars against Carthage, spanning roughly a century and ending in Carthage's annihilation, did more than eliminate a rival. They inflated the scale of what the family-model of control could plausibly manage. Wealth, slaves, and provincial administration arrived faster than the old Republican institutions, designed for a city-state, could absorb them.[10] This is the essay's necessary bridge, not because the Punic Wars are dramatic, though they are, but because they are the moment the family-ego of early Rome begins to monetize itself past the point any single paterfamilias could actually oversee. What had been a household logic became an imperial one, and the strain of that transition is the entire remaining subject of this essay.

Act III · The Fratricide ReturnsLate Republic to Augustus
Political Brothers · The Foreign Catalyst · Monopoly

Octavian, Like Romulus,
Eliminates the Rival.

The blood need not be literal for the pattern to run to its conclusion.

01 ~ The Purest Re-Enactment
Mark Antony vs. Octavian

Caesar plays the father, murdered not by a rival brother but by a collective of senatorial brothers acting, in their own account, to save the res publica from exactly the kind of monopolized fraternal power this essay has been tracing since Romulus.[11] Antony is Caesar's lieutenant. Octavian is his adopted grandnephew, co-heir to the same dignitas, the same claim to be Caesar's true political son. They are not blood brothers. Ronald Syme's classic account of the Augustan settlement treats their rivalry as exactly the kind of structural repetition this essay is arguing for, two men, each the legitimate heir by a different claim, unable to share the inheritance any more than Romulus and Remus could share the hill.[12] Octavian wins the way Romulus won. The other claimant does not survive the outcome.

02 ~ The Catalyst
Cleopatra

Caesar's alliance with Cleopatra was political, a calculation about Egyptian grain and Egyptian gold.[13] Antony's was something else. He set aside Octavia, Octavian's own sister, for a foreign queen, and Adrian Goldsworthy's account of the relationship documents how thoroughly this let Octavian reframe a Roman civil war as something more useful: not brother against brother, which was the truth and the oldest Roman anxiety there was, but Rome against Egypt, civilization against decadence.[14] Cleopatra did not dissolve the Roman family. She gave Octavian the vocabulary to pretend the family had never been the problem in the first place, that the enemy was foreign after all, the way it had always, comfortably, been foreign since the Sabines.

03 ~ The Solution
Augustus's Monopoly

Having won, Augustus did not restore competing paterfamilias to the Republic. He absorbed the role entirely into one family, the Julio-Claudians, and became, in practice if not always in his own careful public language, the only father Rome was permitted to have.[12] The fratricidal impulse did not disappear. It relocated. Suetonius's biographies of Augustus's successors record the resulting pattern with a thoroughness that reads, at this distance, almost clinical: Tiberius's suspicion of his own family, Caligula's purges, Nero's murder of his mother.[15] The public no longer watched brothers kill brothers in a field outside the city. It happened behind palace walls now, which is not the same as it stopping. It is the same crime, given better acoustics.

Act IV · The Institutionalized KnifeThe Empire's Decline
The Limit of Adoption · Collective Murder · The Blood Code's Return

You Cannot Own
a Man's Primal Kinship.

Every mechanism Rome built to absorb outsiders eventually met the one thing it could not absorb.

01 ~ The System's Structural Limit
Arminius and the Teutoburg Forest, 9 CE

Arminius was Germanic by birth and Roman by upbringing, a hostage raised in Rome, granted citizenship, given equestrian rank and a military command, in effect an adopted son of the empire's client system.[16] His own people, the Cherusci, called him back, and he answered by luring three Roman legions under Publius Quinctilius Varus into the forest and destroying them, an event Tacitus and Cassius Dio both record as one of the worst military disasters in Roman history.[17] Peter Wells's modern account situates the disaster precisely where this essay's argument requires it: not as a failure of Roman competence, but as the discovery of a genuine limit to Rome's absorption logic.[18] You can grant a man citizenship. You cannot guarantee which family he decides is the one that actually owns him.

02 ~ Fratricide, Made Corporate
The Praetorian Guard

Created to protect the emperor's body, the Praetorian Guard discovered, well before the first century was out, that a body they could protect was also a body they could dispose of. Cassius Dio records the culmination of this discovery in 193 CE with a scene that reads, even in translation, like satire: the Guard, having murdered Pertinax, put the empire itself up for auction, and Didius Julianus bought it.[19] Fergus Millar's study of imperial power notes that this was not an aberration but the exposure of a structural fact the Guard had always held: whoever controls proximity to the paterfamilias controls the paterfamilias.[20] The individual fratricide of Romulus and Remus had, by this point, become a standing institutional procedure, a collective rather than a single killer, but the transaction was identical. Brothers, or their institutional equivalent, decide who gets to be king by deciding who gets to die.

03 ~ The Reassertion
The Antonines and Commodus

Between 96 and 180 CE, five emperors, Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, and Marcus Aurelius, each adopted a successor chosen for competence rather than blood, and Edward Gibbon, writing more than sixteen centuries later, called the resulting period the time in which the human race was most happy and prosperous, a judgment historians have qualified since but never entirely dismissed.[21] Then Marcus Aurelius broke the pattern and gave the throne to his biological son. Commodus, Cassius Dio records without apparent editorializing that the fact required any, entered the arena himself and fought as a gladiator, the emperor personally performing the exact ritual this essay has argued the empire built specifically to manage its own founding trauma at a safe remove.[22] The system had, for eighty-four years, escaped the blood code through the accident of adoption. The moment blood was available again, Rome reached for it, even though the historical record makes unusually clear that doing so was a catastrophe by every available measure.

Act V · The Empire of the SurvivorEast and West, and Beyond
Council of Brothers · New Origin · The Weaponized Name · Survival

The Surviving Brother
Just Moved East
and Put On a Priest's Robe.

Rome did not end in 476. It relocated, renamed itself, and outlasted its own funeral by nearly a thousand years.

01 ~ The Admission
The Tetrarchy

Diocletian's creation of a four-way division of imperial power, two senior Augusti and two junior Caesars, was an open admission that one man could no longer hold what Augustus had monopolized.[23] Simon Corcoran's study of the Tetrarchic system notes it was a deliberate attempt to replace the single paterfamilias with a governed council of brothers, and that it collapsed into civil war within a generation of Diocletian's own retirement.[24] The attempt failed. The diagnosis behind it, that the empire had outgrown any single family's capacity to hold it, did not.

02 ~ A New Origin, the Same Mechanism
Constantine and Constantinople

Constantine's new capital claimed a different founding story entirely, Christ rather than Romulus, a city consecrated rather than built on a brother's corpse.[25] But the underlying structure did not change with the theology. The emperor remained the sole paterfamilias of the Roman world; he had simply acquired, in Judith Herrin's phrase, divine backing for the role rather than merely martial backing.[26] A new origin story does not dissolve an old mechanism. It gives the mechanism better credentials.

03 ~ The Weapon of Naming
The "Byzantine" Anachronism

The people historians now call Byzantines called themselves Rhomaioi, Romans, without interruption, until Constantinople finally fell in 1453.[27] The term "Byzantine Empire" was coined more than a century after that fall, by the sixteenth-century German historian Hieronymus Wolf, who needed a label for his 1557 compilation of Eastern sources and reached for the city's ancient Greek name, Byzantion, rather than the name its own inhabitants had used for over a thousand years.[28] Anthony Kaldellis's study of the Eastern empire's actual political self-understanding argues this renaming was not neutral scholarship. It let the West, the Papacy, the Holy Roman Empire under Charlemagne, claim sole inheritance of Rome's legitimacy while quietly erasing the competing claim of the people who had never stopped calling themselves Roman.[26] The knife, by this point, did not need to be literal. A name, applied retroactively by someone with no stake in the empire it described, can finish an argument the empire itself was never asked to join.

04 ~ The Ironic Ending
The Fall of the West, 476 CE

The last emperor of the Western Roman Empire was a boy named Romulus Augustulus, deposed by the Germanic general Odoacer without, by most ancient accounts, much ceremony or bloodshed at all.[29] I do not think the Romans who recorded this detail missed what it meant. The empire of the surviving brother ended with a child who carried, diminished into a mocking diminutive, the founder's own name. The wound did not heal. It simply ran out of West to bleed into.

05 ~ The Long Survival
Constantinople to 1453

The Eastern empire outlived the Western one by nearly a millennium, and I think the reason is precisely the theological upgrade described above. The emperor was no longer merely a paterfamilias who could, in principle, be challenged by a strong enough rival brother. He was God's vicar, and to challenge him was not treason alone but heresy, a category with considerably less room for negotiation.[26] The schism between Rome and Constantinople, the iconoclast controversies, the catastrophe of the Fourth Crusade in 1204, when supposed Christian allies sacked the Christian capital of the Christian East, are the same family-ego this essay has traced from the Palatine, now conducted in theological rather than military vocabulary.[30] Constantinople finally fell to the Ottomans in 1453. It had, by then, called itself Rome for longer than Rome itself had stood before it.

Coda

I do not think Rome is unusual for having been built this way. I think Rome is unusual only for having written it down, repeated it, staged it in an arena for eight hundred years, and left us enough of the paperwork to notice. Every empire absorbs outsiders and calls it civilization. Every dynasty eventually kills its way to a succession and calls it stability. Every institution that outgrows its founders finds a new name for the old mechanism and calls the renaming progress. Rome simply told us, at the very beginning, exactly what it was going to do ~ and then did it, methodically, for fourteen centuries, until there was no one left who remembered it any other way.

Act VI · The ObjectionThe Auto-Critique
Infrastructure · The Pax Romana · Material Reality

Aqueducts Are Not
Metaphors.

To reduce Rome to a psychological pathology is a neat literary trick ~ but it requires ignoring the concrete.

01 ~ The False Equivalence
Psychoanalyzing an Empire

I am, of course, psychoanalyzing an empire ~ an inherently fraudulent exercise. To claim that Rome is nothing but a fratricidal wound scaled up to continental proportions is to engage in rhetorical sleight-of-hand. It dismisses the *Pax Romana* ~ two centuries where a Mediterranean basin, historically addicted to tribal slaughter, experienced unprecedented peace, integrated trade, and rising life expectancies. A psychological "family-ego" does not build fifty thousand miles of paved roads. A trauma response does not codify a system of civic law that remains the bedrock of European jurisprudence two millennia later. By obsessing over the violence at the apex, I have conveniently ignored the stabilization at the base.

02 ~ The Pragmatic Filter
The Necessity of Monopoly

The Praetorian murders and succession crises were not the point of the empire ~ they were the localized cost of doing business in a pre-constitutional world. The mechanism of violent absorption was not a "crime" ~ it was the only historically viable technology for breaking local blood-feuds and replacing them with a universal civic identity. If the alternative to Augustus is the perpetual civil war of the late Republic, then Augustus's monopoly is not a pathology. It is a cure. I have condemned the Romans for shedding blood to hold power, while omitting the cold fact that every alternative available to them would have shed infinitely more.

Act VII · The Open Question

If scale strictly requires absorption, and absorption necessitates a crime ~ is it possible to build a civilization that does not begin with a brother on the floor?
