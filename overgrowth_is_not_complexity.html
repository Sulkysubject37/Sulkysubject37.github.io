WHY GOOD SYSTEMS DIE?

WHY GOOD

SYSTEMS

DIE?

Category Institutional Sociology
Focus Systemic Rigidity
Status Peer Review
Abstract — This paper addresses a specific explanatory gap in the study of collective dysfunction: existing frameworks describe how systems become stuck but not why they remain stuck once the actors inside them have recognized the dysfunction. It proposes that three interacting variables, structural complexity, symbolic attachment, and power asymmetry, jointly produce a self-stabilizing condition termed Rigid Equilibrium, in which locally rational agents collectively reproduce a system that has decoupled from its originating function. The paper introduces two boundary conditions, modularity and environmental selection pressure, that determine whether accumulated complexity remains adaptive or becomes overgrown, proposes an agent-based model capable of generating a testable phase transition between these states, traces the mechanism through two deliberately dissimilar cases, and closes by specifying the conditions under which the entire hypothesis would be falsified.

I. The Problem: The Paradox of Intelligent Collectives

The phenomenon under investigation is not that individuals inside large systems behave irrationally. It is that systems composed of individuals behaving rationally—within their available information and local incentives—produce collective outcomes no participant would endorse. This strict separation between micro-level rationality and macro-level dysfunction is the paper's starting boundary.

A caseworker closing a file to meet a quota, a department head protecting a budget line, or a committee member avoiding social friction are each optimizing a locally legible utility function. None of these decisions requires attributing stupidity or malice to the actor. Thomas Schelling's demonstration of residential segregation provides the clearest formal proof: this separation is not an anomaly; it is structurally inevitable in systems built from locally optimizing agents.

Comparative biological systems sharpen this point. Ant colonies coordinate highly complex tasks through decentralized, locally reactive rules without any individual possessing a global representation. Deborah Gordon's behavioral studies document colonies reorganizing fluidly in response to environmental change, entirely avoiding the persistent misallocation that concerns this paper.

Similarly, Suzanne Simard's research on mycorrhizal fungal networks reveals extensive, interdependent complexity that remains highly adaptive. These biological systems possess complexity without producing rigidity. Therefore, complexity alone is not the causal driver. Human systems differ by introducing variables biological collectives lack: symbolic attachment to specific configurations, and asymmetric power to block their revision. Complexity is a necessary condition for the phenomenon. It is not sufficient.

The Gap in the Literature

Existing theories of collective dysfunction are not wrong; they are incomplete. Irving Janis's groupthink explains decision formation, not decision persistence after dissent has surfaced. Herbert Simon's bounded rationality explains why suboptimal decisions are made initially, but not why they remain uncorrected once the suboptimality is recognized. Paul David and W. Brian Arthur's path dependence explains why early choices constrain later ones, but leaves open why those constraints do not weaken when their costs become universally visible.

The theoretical gap across all three literatures is identical: they describe how systems get stuck, but they fail to explain why systems remain stuck once the actors inside them recognize the dysfunction. This paper argues that persistence requires a specific interaction. Epistemic opacity (Complexity), symbolic sunk costs (Attachment), and distributional vetoes (Power Asymmetry) combine into a self-stabilizing loop. Pure cognitive biases cannot explain this persistence; this structural loop can.

II. Human Creations as Evolving Systems

The unit of analysis this paper requires is broader than any single domain term, institution, technology, or ideology, would capture alone, and is defined here as: a persistent, socially transmitted structure produced through cumulative human activity, maintained across more than one generation of participants, and modified incrementally rather than redesigned from a blank state. Institutions, technologies, ideologies, and formal and informal norms all satisfy this definition, and the paper treats them as instances of a single underlying category rather than as separately theorized phenomena, following the broader tradition in cultural evolutionary theory, particularly Robert Boyd and Peter Richerson's account of culture as a system of socially transmitted information subject to variation, selection, and transmission dynamics structurally analogous to, though not identical with, biological evolution. Douglass North's treatment of institutions as the humanly devised constraints that structure political, economic, and social interaction supplies the paper's working definition of the institutional subset of this category specifically.

These creations move through an identifiable lifecycle: creation, in which a structure is deliberately designed or informally originated to address a specific function; transmission, in which the structure is communicated to new participants who did not take part in its original design; accumulation, in which successive participants add rules, exceptions, and elaborations in response to particular local problems; modification, in which the structure is altered, usually incrementally, in response to feedback; and persistence, in which the structure continues to be reproduced by participants long after its original designers have left the system or died.

The paper's necessary ontological commitment, following Robert Merton's account of unanticipated consequences of purposive social action, is that creations acquire emergent properties not represented in, and frequently in direct tension with, the intentions of their founders. This licenses treating creations as quasi-organisms possessing their own trajectories of growth, adaptation, and decay, without committing to the stronger and considerably less defensible claim that creations possess intrinsic purposes or that their historical trajectory was in any sense destined. The quasi-organism framing is a methodological device for tracking emergent, path-dependent dynamics, not a teleological claim about the creation possessing agency or a predetermined developmental endpoint. A bureaucracy that has drifted from its founding mission has not been pursuing a hidden goal of its own. It has been reproduced, incrementally and locally, by successive participants each solving a locally scoped problem, and the aggregate trajectory of those local solutions is what this paper tracks.

III. The Core Variables: Complexity, Attachment, and Power

Three variables, and a fourth introduced as a necessary correction to the first three, constitute the theoretical machinery this paper requires before the central hypothesis can be stated.

Complexity (C)

Defined as the number and interdependence of a system's components, rules, and informational dependencies. Herbert Simon's account of hierarchically structured complex systems, and his concept of near-decomposability, supplies this paper's formal treatment of complexity. Complexity is a structural property of the system, not a subjective impression of difficulty.

Attachment (A)

Defined as the psychological, social, and symbolic cost associated with abandoning a creation. Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer's demonstration of the sunk cost effect supplies the psychological mechanism. Philip Tetlock's research on sacred values extends the mechanism into a specifically symbolic and identity-linked register.

Functional Decoupling (D)

Defined as the divergence between a creation's originating function, the problem it was built to solve, and the actual output its current reproduction system optimizes for. A hospital system originally built to support patient care that comes to optimize primarily for billing code accuracy exhibits decoupling in this precise sense.

Power Asymmetry, or Distributional Veto (P)

Corrects an incompleteness in treating complexity and attachment as sufficient on their own. Mancur Olson's account of distributional coalitions demonstrates that specific actors frequently possess veto power over simplification precisely because they capture rents generated by the system's opacity. Anne Krueger's formalization of rent-seeking supplies the formal economic vocabulary. The inclusion of Power moves the theory from a purely cognitive and structural account toward a political economic one: complexity is actively defended by specific actors who benefit from its persistence.

IV. The Overgrowth Hypothesis

Overgrowth is defined as the state in which accumulated Complexity, Attachment, and Power Asymmetry substantially exceed the requirements necessary to perform a creation's originating function, producing high resistance to modification.

This definition requires an immediate and load-bearing distinction: Overgrowth is not equivalent to Complexity. A system can possess extremely high complexity and remain fully adaptive. The divergence is governed by two boundary conditions treated here as necessary interaction terms.

  • Modularity (M): The degree to which components can be modified or replaced independently. Following Baldwin and Clark's formalization of modularity in technical systems, high modularity allows a system to accumulate internal complexity safely, because the cost of modifying one component does not propagate uncontrollably.
  • Environmental Selection Pressure (S): The intensity of external existential pressure to perform the originating function correctly. Following Nelson and Winter's evolutionary theory of economic change, high selection pressure ensures overgrowth is continuously pruned, as the cost of dysfunction cannot be externalized onto a captive constituency.

The interaction of these variables yields the paper's central formal axioms:

Axiom I: The Adaptive Creation

High Complexity (C) + High Modularity (M) → Increased Capability

Complexity scales functional capacity without introducing rigidity.

Axiom II: The Overgrown Creation

High Complexity (C) + Low Modularity (M) + Low Selection Pressure (S) → Rigidity and Decoupling

Complexity converts into structural overgrowth because the mechanisms of independent modification and external correction are both absent.

This interaction structure makes the hypothesis actionable. It specifies the precise conditions under which complexity becomes pathogenic, identifying the two intervention points (increasing M or increasing S) through which overgrowth can be reversed.

V. The Rigid Equilibrium

Rigid Equilibrium is defined as a dynamically stable macrostate in which locally rational behaviors, an employee saving personal time by following an established procedure rather than proposing its revision, a department protecting its budget allocation rather than volunteering it for elimination, a committee member avoiding the social cost of raising an objection, collectively reproduce a functionally decoupled system, without requiring that any participant in that reproduction be acting irrationally at the local level at which their decision is made.

The prior formulation of this state as a "Moronic Equilibrium" is explicitly rejected. Since individual irrationality is not a prerequisite for the state's emergence, labeling the resulting system state as irrational introduces a direct contradiction into the theory's premises. The system is rigid precisely because its participants are behaving rationally given the information and constraints locally available to them.

What stabilizes this equilibrium once it has formed is a specific feedback structure connecting the three core variables from Section III. Complexity increases the search cost of locating and evaluating alternative arrangements, a mechanism directly continuous with Simon's bounded rationality: as the number of interdependent components rises, the cost of fully modeling the consequences of any proposed change rises correspondingly, and agents facing this cost rationally default to satisficing within the existing arrangement rather than searching exhaustively for a superior one. Attachment increases the subjective penalty associated with switching to an alternative arrangement, independent of that alternative's objective merits, consistent with Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch, and Richard Thaler's demonstration that loss aversion and status quo bias systematically inflate the perceived cost of departing from an existing reference point relative to the cost of adopting it in the first place. Power allows elites who benefit from the existing arrangement to externalize the costs of reform onto the periphery, a distributional dynamic already established in Olson's account of coalition capture, meaning that even where search costs and switching penalties could in principle be overcome, the actors with the greatest capacity to initiate reform frequently bear the least of its cost from continued dysfunction and the most of its cost from successful change.

Paul Pierson's account of increasing returns in political institutions supplies the formal closure of this loop: once an institutional arrangement has been in place long enough to generate the complexity, attachment, and captured constituencies described above, each of these three variables independently raises the cost of the alternative path relative to the cost of continuing along the existing one, producing a self-reinforcing dynamic in which the probability of continued reproduction of the arrangement increases over time even as the case for reform, evaluated on its substantive merits, does not weaken. Rigid Equilibrium is this dynamic made explicit: not a failure of any individual participant's reasoning, but a stable aggregate outcome of individually reasonable participants operating inside a structure whose feedback loops reward continued local compliance over collective correction.

VI. The Generality Problem: Two Deep Cases

A framework intended to generalize across radically different domains cannot be adequately tested by a wide, shallow survey of superficially similar cases. This section instead performs a most-different-systems comparison, selecting two cases that share almost no surface features, one bureaucratic-technical, one cultural-cognitive, on the premise that if the same causal mechanism can be traced through both, the mechanism is more likely to be tracking a genuine structural regularity rather than a domain-specific artifact.

CASE_A

The VA Healthcare Scheduling System

In 2014, the VA Office of Inspector General documented systematic manipulation of patient wait-time data, including the use of unofficial waiting lists to conceal true wait intervals. Kizer and Jha's analysis situates this failure within a longer structural pattern: a scheduling apparatus overloaded with decades of congressionally mandated performance metrics and eligibility rules. The system became so opaque that administrators could satisfy audit metrics without delivering the underlying clinical care.

The structural decay of the VA system traces precisely along the proposed mechanism:

  • Complexity (C) → Opacity: Overlapping congressional mandates and reporting requirements accumulated across decades, rendering the actual patient queue invisible to outside evaluators.
  • Opacity → Power Asymmetry (P): Congress and auditors became entirely reliant on internal facility leadership to interpret and report the metrics, concentrating interpretive authority into a distributional veto.
  • Power → Attachment (A): Civil service protections and congressional appropriation cycles raised the political switching cost of any structural reform to unacceptable levels.
  • Attachment → Decoupling (D): The system stabilized in a Rigid Equilibrium, successfully optimizing its internal audit metrics while simultaneously failing its originating function of veteran care.
CASE_B

Mainline Protestant Denominations

Dean Kelley documented a severe divergence in the mid-twentieth century: Mainline denominations experienced sustained membership decline, while denominations demanding strict theological commitment grew. Finke and Stark attribute this to differential adaptive capacity. Mainline denominations had accumulated massive theological ambiguity (Complexity) and layered institutional bureaucracy. Because affiliation was historically inherited rather than chosen, they faced low selection pressure. As the environment shifted toward a competitive religious marketplace, they failed to adapt. Wuthnow's analysis confirms that denominational elites possessed disproportionate influence over reform and lacked incentives to dismantle their own institutional structures.

Despite sharing no surface features with a healthcare bureaucracy, the exact same feedback loop governs the religious decline:

  • Complexity (C) → Opacity: Theological and institutional ambiguity accumulated across decades, producing systemic opacity regarding which structural features were driving congregational collapse.
  • Opacity → Power Asymmetry (P): This ambiguity forced reliance on denominational experts and seminaries to interpret the decline, granting elites a structural veto over reforms.
  • Power → Attachment (A): Elite veto power combined with the deeply entrenched identity-attachment of the aging core membership to generate insurmountable switching costs.
  • Attachment → Decoupling (D): Denominational bureaucracies continued to operate and reproduce themselves, entirely decoupled from their originating function of sustaining religious practice.

VII. Formalization and Falsification

The theory as stated in Sections III through V is precise enough to be computationally implemented, and this section specifies that implementation rather than resting on the vaguer algebraic formulation the theory's earlier development relied on. Following the methodological precedent established by Joshua Epstein and Robert Axtell's foundational demonstration that emergent social regularities can be generated from simple rule-governed agent interactions without requiring top-down specification of the aggregate outcome, the theory is formalized as an agent-based model.

The model utilizes a Networked Agent-Based architecture. Agents operate under bounded rationality, evaluating a locally sampled subset of rule configurations rather than optimizing globally. The environment contains a global optimum (the originating function) embedded in a fitness landscape.

The decision of an agent i to adopt a new rule configuration j is governed by the following utility function:

$$ U_{i \to j} = \big[ V_j - S(C) - \alpha A_i + \gamma N_j \big] \times (1 - \beta P) $$

Model Variables

  • $V_j$ is the objective payoff of the proposed rule configuration.
  • $S(C)$ is the search cost to evaluate the rule, which scales directly with systemic Complexity ($C$).
  • $A_i$ is the symbolic switching cost (Attachment) penalized by a coefficient $\alpha$, independent of the rule's objective merit.
  • $N_j$ is the network contagion term, representing the proportion of local peers adopting the reform, scaled by $\gamma$.
  • $P$ is the distributional veto imposed by high-status actors (Power Asymmetry) who block reforms threatening their structural rents, scaled by $\beta \in [0, 1]$. As $\beta P \to 1$, the utility of adoption collapses to zero.

The Formal Prediction: A Phase Transition

The model predicts a nonlinear phase transition, aligning with Granovetter's threshold models of collective behavior. Below a critical threshold of the combined parameters ($C, A, P$), the population absorbs exogenous shocks and successfully navigates the landscape toward the global optimum.

Once the combined tax of search costs, symbolic attachment, and elite veto exceeds the marginal objective benefit and social momentum ($S(C) + \alpha A_i > V_j + \gamma N_j$, or $\beta P \to 1$), the population undergoes a phase transition. It stabilizes in a mathematically inevitable, locally optimal basin. The system is now locked in a Rigid Equilibrium—stable against local search, yet globally suboptimal and permanently decoupled from its originating function.

Following Karl Popper's criterion that a theory's scientific standing depends on the specification of the conditions under which it could be shown false, the hypothesis is falsified under any of the following three conditions. First, the theory fails if increasing Complexity is observed to correlate consistently with faster, rather than slower, adaptation in environments independently characterized by low Modularity, since this would directly contradict the interaction structure specified in Section IV. Second, the theory fails if powerful actors, high-Power agents in the model's terms, are observed to actively promote simplification and transparency in the absence of exogenous pressure forcing them to do so, since the theory's account of Power depends specifically on high-status actors having a rational incentive to preserve, rather than dismantle, the opacity from which they benefit. Third, the theory fails if highly Attached populations are observed to demonstrate higher rates of adaptive correction than low-Attachment populations during systemic shocks, a direct reversal of the causal direction the theory predicts between Attachment and adaptive rigidity. Each of these three conditions specifies an empirically observable pattern whose occurrence the theory, as stated, positively excludes.

VIII. Conclusion: The Overgrowth Hypothesis

This paper has developed a unified mechanism: Complexity combined with Attachment and Power Asymmetry produces Opacity. Opacity stabilizes the Rigid Equilibrium. The Rigid Equilibrium produces Functional Decoupling. This framework synthesizes institutional sociology, behavioral economics, and political science into a single causal chain. Through the boundary conditions of Modularity and Selection Pressure, it specifies the exact structural circumstances under which this chain is activated.

What this paper defends is The Overgrowth Hypothesis, stated formally as follows:

"For human-created systems, increasing structural complexity, institutional embedding, and power asymmetries tend, beyond a context-dependent threshold (defined by low modularity and low selection pressure), to exponentially increase the probability of functional decoupling and resistance to corrective change."

This is a probabilistic tendency rooted in the dynamics of complex adaptive systems, not a deterministic law of nature. The falsification conditions specified in Section VII provide the binding empirical terms under which this claim could be shown to fail.

The paper's contribution is specific: it explains why systems remain stuck once their dysfunction has been recognized by the actors inside them. This is an explanatory target that groupthink, bounded rationality, and path dependence cannot reach alone. The Overgrowth Hypothesis maps the structural persistence of failure. Crucially, it identifies the two precise levers—increasing the independent modifiability of components, or restoring genuine external correction—through which that persistence could theoretically be broken.
